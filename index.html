About me

I am Zhao Siyan, a first-semester MSc Cyber Security student at Universiti Malaya, having completed a BEng in Computer Science at Shenzhen University. I am also a research intern at the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology (SIAT), where I build security prototypes at the meeting point of machine learning and defence: traffic drift detection, DoH tunnel detection, open-set API anomaly detection and audit-log forensics.

I came to cybersecurity from the detection side. Building models that flag anomalies taught me a hard lesson: a detector is only as good as the evidence it sees, and models alone cannot defend a network. A defender also needs the packet-level, operating-system and human view of a system — exactly what this course teaches hands-on.

This portfolio documents that growth with verifiable evidence. Every project states its objective, scope, method, results and limitations. I value honest, reproducible work: fixed seeds, declared failure cases, and evidence a reviewer can check independently.

05

Learning journey

I started WQE7001 with a detection-and-machine-learning background and almost no packet-level experience. The course is rebuilding my view of a network from the frame upward.

Two topics stand out. The first is the Linux and packet-analysis sequence (Modules 4, 5, 8 and 9). The shell labs, and then the Wireshark labs on Ethernet frames and the TCP three-way handshake, made the transport layer concrete. Before, I knew TCP from diagrams; now I can open a capture and read who sent what, whether a reply really answers the frame it claims to, and why the flags matter. That skill paid off immediately: I later wrote a Scapy tool that builds raw SYN probes and classifies replies, and the only reason I could debug it was that I could read the capture.

The second is reconnaissance and information asymmetry (Modules 13 and 15, reinforced by a Week-4 footprint lab in my penetration course). Searching crt.sh for a university domain returned 232 unique hostnames, including VPN, test, admin and GitLab hosts that appear nowhere on the public website. WHOIS exposed a thirty-year-old domain with redacted contacts; MX records revealed the mail topology; one sample photo's EXIF leaked GPS coordinates, the camera model and the author. The lesson: an attacker does not need to touch your systems to map them, and defenders cannot stop the looking — they can only make sure there is less to find.

One thing went wrong in my own lab, and diagnosing it taught me more than the success case. While validating my SYN scanner's capture, the file showed roughly twice as many RST packets as the tool had sent. I suspected a classification bug, but an independent Nmap run showed the same doubling. The real cause was the Linux kernel on the scanner host: it also receives the SYN/ACKs and answers them with its own RSTs, because the socket layer never expected them. The fix was not code but honesty — document the artifact, keep the tool's closing RSTs on a bounded schedule, and disclose the doubling in the report instead of hiding it. When the capture contradicts the code, check what the operating system is doing underneath the code.

What I still find challenging is web application exploitation. My background is detection and machine learning, so OWASP-style attacks — injection, broken authentication, cross-site scripting — are my weakest area, and spotting the flaw in a lab still feels a long way from spotting the attacker in real traffic.

The concrete goal follows from that gap: complete a 12-week web security foundation plan (4–6 hours per week of structured labs on legal practice platforms) by 28 February 2027, and enter a first beginner CTF. I will measure it with two numbers: every planned lab completed, and at least one CTF attempt written up publicly in this portfolio. When both are true, I will update this section with the evidence.
